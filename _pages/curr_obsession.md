Spoken word recognition (and to some extent, speech perception) are largely characterized as processes which closely follow the temporal sequence of the input. For instance, it is well accepted that people begin to use speech information to make inferences about the identity of a spoken word **immediately** upon perception, and that these inferences are updated **incrementally** as soon as new information arrives. This proposes that the system of spoken word recognition is strongly tied to the timing of when speech input arrives--an assumption that is embedded in many computational models. 

However, over the past ~10 years research has found that the system does not always work this way. Some work ([1](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/cogs.12700), [2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-019-01712-9), [3](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2027-81136-001)) suggests that when listeners hear fricatives like /s/ and /ʃ/ they do not immediately use the relevant acoustic cues to recognize what sound they are hearing. Other work ([4](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0010027717302378), [5](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-51514-3)) has found that some listeners with hearing loss do not recognize words immediately upon the perception of speech, and in fact, that even normal hearing listeners can be described as existing along a continuum of immediacy ([5](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-51514-3), [6](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2026-31269-001)). Additional findings have found that listeners consider interpretations of speech which match both the canonical order of the input (e.g., *cat*) and the anadrome of this input (*tack*), suggesting that the timing of the sequence does not (fully) constrain processing ([7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-013-0417-0), [8](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1747021819849512), [9](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/full/10.1044/2022_JSLHR-22-00231?casa_token=LykMJk-vkmkAAAAA%3AHLA7FaX27ZAEAE83rY2xxFiwYEgXNcUP7Q-qS_MmjRuU5lreOMC-la8YBoNhEkgW9EzhVPtvnPtvwg), [10](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/17470218211056842)).

This questions our current theories of language comprehension, and provides a new set of benchmarks with which to evaluate computational models: Do they show anadrome effects? Can they simulate fricative recognition? If so, how does this inform our theoretical understanding, and if not, what architectures are required to do so? My colleagues [Hyoju Kim](https://sites.google.com/view/hyojus-webpage/home), [Bob McMurray](https://psychology.uiowa.edu/people/bob-mcmurray), and I wrote a [reivew](https://www.cell.com/trends/cognitive-sciences/fulltext/S1364-6613(25)00144-5) all about this, and I am actively working with current models and developing new ones to examine these questions.
